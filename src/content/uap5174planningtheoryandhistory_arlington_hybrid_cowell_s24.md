## Course Description
This course provides an overview of key planning theories, planning history, and planning processes and approaches. It equips students with conceptual tools to improve the quality of deliberation and develop a critical perspective on challenges facing contemporary urban planning. Through readings, lectures, discussions, debates, assignments, and research projects, students engage with the intellectual foundations and ethical dimensions of the planning profession.

## Learning Objectives
- Compare and contrast different planning philosophies and theories (e.g., rational/comprehensive, incremental, communicative, advocacy)
- Articulate and critique various planning theories
- Evaluate the applicability of different planning theories to planning practice and contemporary planning questions
- Analyze important events, themes, and debates in the history and evolution of planning and examine their influence on current planning thought and practice
- Examine the different ways ethical dilemmas can be framed and addressed in specific cases
- Communicate information effectively and efficiently via memos, oral presentations, and other formats

## Course Topics and Schedule
- **Urban culture, society, and the city** — foundational sociological perspectives on urban life (Simmel, Wirth, Mumford, Anderson)
- **Planning history and visions** — utopian planners, racial inequality, and early planning ideas (Howard, Le Corbusier, Wright, Olmsted, Thomas)
- **Foundations of 20th-century planning theory** — authoritarian modernism, capitalism, zoning, and critiques (Scott, Jacobs, Foglesong, Marcuse, Hirt)
- **Goals, justifications, and critiques of planning (Part I)** — rationalism, incrementalism, neoliberalism (Healey, Amin, Klosterman, Lindblom)
- **Goals, justifications, and critiques of planning (Part II)** — sustainability, resilience, spatial justice, cosmopolitan urbanism (Campbell, Fainstein, Young, Sandercock)
- **Implications of practice for theory** — community development, minority planners, gentrification, and equity in specific city contexts
- **Collaborative and participatory planning theories** — citizen participation, advocacy planning, community land trusts (Davidoff, Forester, Fischer, Arnstein)
- **Planning ethics** — professional ethics, AICP Code of Ethics, the Graduate Honor Code (Wachs, Thomas)
- **Indigenous and insurgent planning histories and theories** — Global South perspectives, insurgent planning, indigenous planning traditions (Miraftab, Watson, Shatkin, Jojola)
- **Debates and final presentations** — structured debates on topics including smart growth, gentrification, the American Dream, car-free cities, smart cities, and single-family zoning; group final project presentations

## Advising Notes
UAP 5174 is the foundational planning theory and history requirement for the MURP program and is essential for all incoming students, providing the conceptual vocabulary and historical context that underpins all subsequent coursework. This section, taught by Dr. Margaret Cowell at the Arlington/NCR campus, uses a hybrid-flipped format that balances in-person discussion with asynchronous reading and reflection work, making it well-suited for working professionals in the Northern Virginia area. The course places notable emphasis on race, equity, and justice in planning history and theory alongside canonical planning frameworks, preparing students to engage critically with both scholarship and practice. Students should plan to take this course early in their program, as its theoretical grounding supports studio courses, electives, and the capstone; no formal prerequisites are listed, but it is designed as a graduate-level introduction to the discipline.
