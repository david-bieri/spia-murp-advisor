## Course Description
This course explores the concept of power as the foundation for understanding US politics and policy. It examines questions about the nature, sources, limitations, and distribution of power, and how those distributions both explain policy outcomes and are shaped by them. Students engage with a range of analytic frameworks across the semester, culminating in an original power analysis of a chosen policy issue.

## Learning Objectives
- Develop a rigorous conceptual understanding of power and its multiple dimensions (coercive, procedural, transformative, etc.)
- Apply competing theoretical frameworks (pluralism, elite theory, agenda-setting, etc.) to real-world US policy contexts
- Analyze the power relationships underlying a specific public policy issue through original research
- Engage critically with assigned readings and contribute to seminar-style discussion
- Produce graduate-level analytical writing, including a policy proposal and a full power analysis paper

## Course Topics and Schedule
- **Introduction to Power:** Dahl's foundational concept of power; defining and operationalizing power analytically
- **Elite Theory:** Mills's *The Power Elite*; concentrated power structures in American society
- **Pluralism:** Dahl's *Who Governs?*; distributed power and democratic competition
- **Faces/Dimensions of Power:** Bachrach & Baratz's two faces of power; Lukes's radical three-dimensional view
- **Coercive Power:** Schelling on arms and influence; power-dependence relations; coercion theory
- **Procedural Power — Representation and Agenda Setting:** Pitkin, Schattschneider, Cox & McCubbins, presidential leadership of public opinion
- **Transformative Power:** Soft power (Nye), propaganda, mass opinion formation (Zaller), normative change (Finnemore)
- **Liberty, Resistance, and Consent:** Berlin's two concepts of liberty; Scott on hidden transcripts; Arendt on violence
- **Revolution and Structural Change:** Skocpol on social revolutions; Hirschman's exit, voice, and loyalty framework
- **Applied Policy Area Sessions:** Student-led seminars on US Grand Strategy and three additional student-proposed policy areas (weeks 11–14)

## Advising Notes
UAP 5004 is a rigorous theory-focused seminar well suited to MURP students seeking a strong analytical foundation in political power, policy formation, and governance — particularly those with interests in policy analysis, advocacy, or public administration. The course bridges domestic and international dimensions of power, making it valuable for students interested in urban policy, federal programs, or global planning contexts. It is a graduate seminar with no listed prerequisites, but students should be prepared for heavy reading and active participation in discussion; the workload includes substantial primary theory texts alongside an original written policy analysis. Offered at the Arlington/NCR campus by Prof. Levinson, this course complements MURP core requirements in planning theory and policy and would pair well with methods or policy-focused electives.
