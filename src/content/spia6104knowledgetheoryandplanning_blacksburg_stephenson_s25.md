## Course Description
The philosophy of social science is a meta-theoretical field that investigates how humans make sense of their lived social reality, encompassing disciplines such as political science, sociology, anthropology, economics, human geography, and social psychology. The course explores enduring philosophical debates concerning social science inquiry—including questions of ontology, epistemology, human agency, and appropriate methods of understanding human behavior. Students are challenged not to adopt a single answer but to develop their own informed judgments about these foundational theoretical controversies.

---

## Learning Objectives
- Grasp alternative epistemological and ontological positions in the social scientific literature, including debates about community values in research, human reasoning and decision-making, and environmental versus individual determinants of lived experience
- Connect the evolution of philosophical debates concerning the roles and foundations of social science inquiry
- Ascertain the philosophical positions of authors even when those positions are not stated explicitly
- Reflect critically on one's own ontological and epistemological perspectives when designing and conducting research
- Develop independent judgments concerning the ongoing controversies highlighted by this meta-theoretical field

## Course Topics and Schedule
- **Core Concerns and Foundations** (Jan 27–Feb 3): Introduction to philosophy of social science; key epistemological and ontological questions
- **Naturalism, Explanation, and the Nature of Social Inquiry** (Feb 10–17): Taylor on interpretation; Bishop and Rosenberg on positivism and its critics
- **Alternate Perspectives I–III** (Feb 17–Mar 3): Hermeneutics, interpretivism, critical theory, and constructivism; Benton & Craib, Rosenberg, and Cartwright
- **Ethics and Interpretation** (Mar 17–24): Normative dimensions of social science; ethics of inquiry; Cartwright on values and objectivity
- **Recent Arguments in the Field** (Mar 31): Contemporary debates; Kincaid on reduction and social ontology
- **Moral Theory and Virtue Ethics** (Apr 7–14): MacIntyre's *After Virtue* as an exemplar synthesis engaging virtue, narrative, and social practice
- **Critical Realism** (Apr 21): Buch-Hansen and Nielsen on the critical realist framework and its relevance to social inquiry
- **Planning Theory** (Apr 28): Beauregard's *Advanced Introduction to Planning Theory* as a bridge from philosophical foundations to planning practice
- **Course Wrap-Up and Final Discussion** (May 5): Integration of semester themes; preparation for final conceptual analysis

## Advising Notes
SPIA 6104 is a doctoral-level seminar suitable for MURP students with strong interest in the theoretical and philosophical foundations of planning and social science research, particularly those considering academic careers or advanced research roles. The course satisfies graduate curriculum requirements across SPIA programs and the ASPECT program, and its explicit inclusion of Beauregard's *Advanced Introduction to Planning Theory* directly bridges philosophy of social science to planning practice, making it especially relevant to MURP students at the theory end of the curriculum. Students should expect a heavy, variable reading load drawn from seven required texts plus Canvas supplements, and should be prepared for intensive seminar discussion, oral presentations, and substantial analytical writing.
