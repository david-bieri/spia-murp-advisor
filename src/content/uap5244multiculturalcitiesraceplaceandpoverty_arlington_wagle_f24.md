## Course Description
This course examines the relationship between race, place, poverty, and power in urban contexts, exploring how poverty persists alongside redevelopment and is often racialized. Students investigate urban inequalities across topics including housing, public services, environmental justice, education, and public health using racial and economic inequality as a primary lens. The course merges theory and case studies from U.S. and global cities to debate how planners and policymakers can address inequality, race, and place.

## Learning Objectives
- Build a shared understanding of race, poverty, and segregation in urban settings
- Analyze how cities are experienced and imagined differently across communities and racial groups
- Examine the connections between various forms of urban inequality, including housing, transportation, education, environmental justice, and public services
- Critically evaluate how planning and policy decisions create, sustain, or reinforce racial and economic inequalities
- Develop and articulate well-reasoned positions on debates surrounding race, place, and poverty in planning and policy contexts

## Course Topics and Schedule
- **Weeks 1–2:** Introduction; racial categories, urban racial identity, and settler colonialism
- **Week 3:** Conceptualizing city and space; Black geographies and placemaking
- **Week 4:** Race, poverty, and residential segregation; housing taxonomies and school district boundaries
- **Week 5:** Housing, neighborhoods, gentrification, and racialized dispossession
- **Week 6:** Planning theory and practice; racial and reparative planning; multicultural and transformative approaches
- **Week 7:** Public services and racialized access; water, waste, and infrastructure
- **Weeks 9–10:** Transportation equity and education inequality; race and access disparities
- **Week 12:** Environmental justice; neoliberalism, environmental racism, and disaster vulnerability
- **Week 13:** International perspectives; race and inequality in Latin America and South Africa
- **Week 15:** Emerging challenges; climate justice, COVID-19 disparities, and climate gentrification

## Advising Notes
This course is well suited for MURP students with interests in social equity, housing, environmental justice, community development, or diversity and inclusion in planning practice. It functions as a seminar with substantial discussion leadership responsibilities, making it ideal for students who want to deepen their critical thinking and academic writing skills alongside their planning coursework. The course carries no listed prerequisites, but students should expect reading-intensive weeks and be prepared to engage with theory as well as empirical case studies. The midterm and final assignments offer flexibility for students to connect course themes directly to their thesis research or policy concentration, making it a valuable complement to more technical MURP coursework.
