## Course Description
This course offers a broad overview of social ecology as an overarching framework for conceptualizing scientific and societal problems across spatial, temporal, sociocultural, and virtual contexts. It examines how the design of cities, neighborhoods, and buildings affects health and wellbeing; how poverty and inequality shape people's lives; and how information and communication technologies influence quality of life. Students apply social ecological theory and transdisciplinary research methods to analyze complex urban problems and develop evidence-based intervention strategies.

## Learning Objectives
- Examine the conceptual and methodological principles of social ecology and systems theory, including interdependence, multi-level analysis of people-environment transactions, and contextual scope of theory, research, and community intervention
- Analyze research and policy questions from a broad, ecological perspective that integrates multiple disciplines and links basic theory and research with community problem solving
- Appreciate and discern the linkages between the social, political, economic, cultural, and spatial aspects of environments
- Become acquainted with the research methods, tools, and techniques used in the field of social ecology
- Critically evaluate empirical research and findings in the field of social ecology
- Create a research evidence-based product to address an urban issue using a social ecological perspective

## Course Topics and Schedule
- **Weeks 1–2 — Course Overview and Historical Foundations:** Introduction to social ecology; historical origins and conceptual foundations, including human ecology, behavior settings, and sentiment/symbolism as ecological variables
- **Weeks 3–4 — Conceptualizing Social Problems and Systems Thinking:** Sociological approaches to social problems; racial and economic inequality; systems thinking and social ecological research; big data and smart urbanism
- **Week 5 — Confronting Social Problems with Social Ecology:** Transdisciplinary research; community health promotion; social entrepreneurship; the political power of social media *(Problem Definition due)*
- **Week 6 — Student-Led Case Study Discussion:** Urban ecology and nature's services infrastructure (Los Angeles Million Trees Initiative)
- **Week 7 — Environmental Stressors I:** Noise, traffic, crowding, and digital overload; livable streets; mobile device effects on social interaction
- **Week 8 — Environmental Stressors II — Poverty and Inequality:** Social determinants of health; childhood poverty; income inequality and social cohesion; environmental justice *(Systems Map due Week 9)*
- **Week 9 — Health and Illness:** Social networks and obesity; the global obesity pandemic; COVID-19 and American medicine; environmental justice and public health
- **Weeks 10–11 — Sustainability, Justice, and Resilience; Urban Form and Design:** Tragedy of the commons; smart cities; urban form and climate vulnerability; third places; green space and wellbeing; Internet of Things
- **Weeks 12 and final — Student-Led Case Discussions and Final Presentations:** Case studies on double exposure in Phoenix and urban flood vulnerability in Taipei; final plenary panel presentations

## Advising Notes
UAP 5424 Urban Social Ecology is well-suited for MURP students interested in the social dimensions of planning, including health equity, environmental justice, poverty, sustainability, and the built environment's effects on human wellbeing. The course provides a strong theoretical and methodological grounding in social ecological systems thinking that complements more technical planning courses and supports research-oriented concentrations. It is offered at the Arlington/NCR campus in a hybrid format, making it accessible to working professionals and students in the Northern Virginia cohort; students should be prepared for both independent asynchronous work and active synchronous participation. The scaffolded final paper sequence—problem definition, systems map, and a full research paper with plenary presentation—makes this course particularly valuable for students seeking to develop a capstone-ready research skill set or who are interested in transdisciplinary approaches to urban social problems.
