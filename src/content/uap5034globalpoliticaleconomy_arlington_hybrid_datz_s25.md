## Course Description
This graduate seminar explores how politics influences economic policy and why particular economic ideas have guided both private decision-making and public policy design. Drawing on a multidisciplinary perspective spanning political science, sociology, economics, and economic history, the course traces key developments from the Great Depression through contemporary challenges including financial crises, global trade tensions, weaponized interdependence, and the resurgence of national populism. No prior training in economics is required.

---

## Learning Objectives
- Analyze major episodes in the history of global political economy — from the 1929 crash through the 2008 financial crisis — in historical perspective
- Examine the origins and evolution of key multilateral institutions such as the World Bank and IMF
- Evaluate how neoliberalism emerged, spread globally, and has demonstrated resilience
- Assess the causes and consequences of recurrent financial crises, including the role of human behavior
- Analyze contemporary challenges including trade conflicts, weaponized economic interdependence, semiconductor geopolitics, and the rise of national populism
- Apply a multidisciplinary framework drawing on political science, sociology, economics, economic history, and geography

## Course Topics and Schedule
- **The 1929 Crash and the Great Depression:** Stock market collapse, causes of the Depression, and FDR's New Deal response
- **Rebuilding the Post-WWII World Economy:** Bretton Woods system, embedded liberalism, and the origins of the IMF and World Bank
- **The Rise and Spread of Neoliberalism:** Market-driven policy in the U.S. and globally; neoliberal resilience and its political foundations
- **21st-Century Financial Instability:** Boom-bust cycles, behavioral economics, manias and panics as recurring phenomena
- **The 2007–08 Global Financial Crisis:** Origins in the U.S. housing market, global contagion, policy responses, and lessons learned
- **Global Trade at a Crossroads:** Free trade under challenge, the China shock, Trump tariffs, and electoral consequences of trade dislocation
- **Weaponized Interdependence:** How global economic networks are leveraged for state coercion; private infrastructure and geopolitical power
- **The Trade-Security Nexus — Semiconductors:** Chip War dynamics, supply chain vulnerability, and economic statecraft
- **The Reemergence of National Populism:** Left-behind places, inequality, and the domestic and international consequences of populist resurgence
- **Future Prospects:** Democratic backsliding, the future of liberal order, and capitalism's relationship with democracy

## Advising Notes
This course is well suited for MURP students interested in the intersection of economic policy, global governance, and political economy — particularly those focusing on economic development, housing finance, infrastructure investment, or international planning contexts. It provides strong theoretical and historical grounding in how macroeconomic ideas and financial systems shape policy environments that planners must navigate. No economics background is required, making it accessible to students coming from diverse disciplinary backgrounds. The course is offered at the Arlington/NCR campus with Zoom access for remote participants, and features a reading-intensive seminar format with two substantial written assignments; students should plan their workload accordingly.
