# Thesis: Level of Service: Design Standards, Imposed Car Dependence, and Urbanization in the Capitalist State

**Author:** Will Vincent Heltzel
**Year:** 2025
**Degree:** MURP — Plan A
**Advisor:** David S. Bieri
**Committee:** Paroma S. Wagle; Lee Vinsel

## Research Question and Problem Statement

This thesis examines how Level of Service (LOS), a widely used transportation design standard, functions as a mechanism of capitalist spatial logic rather than a neutral technical measure. It asks how federal policy decisions of the mid-twentieth century established a car-centric discourse in American urban transportation planning, and how that discourse is reproduced through standards like LOS to serve the production and consumption priorities of the capitalist state.

## Planning Domain

Transportation Planning; Urban Theory; Land Use; Environmental Planning (streets and public space)

---

## Geographic Focus

United States (national policy focus); case study of US Route 19 in Pasco County, Florida

---

## Research Methods

- Historical document analysis of three federal "watershed moments": the First National Conference on Street and Highway Safety (1924), the Housing Act of 1949, and the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956
- Critical discourse analysis drawing on Marxist urban theory and critical theory
- Analytical framework synthesis combining Lefebvre, Foucault, Harvey, and Soja
- Literature review across three bodies of scholarship: Marxist urban theory, critical theory, and contemporary transportation planning research
- Case study analysis of US 19 in Pasco County, Florida, using highway capacity data and streetview documentation

---

## Key Findings

- LOS, as codified in the Highway Capacity Manual and AASHTO's Green Book and adopted at federal, state, and local levels, prioritises automobile throughput over pedestrian safety, transit service, and urban livability on streets where those needs should take precedence
- Three mid-twentieth century federal policy decisions collectively constructed and institutionalised a car-centric discourse that normalised highway-like design standards for urban streets
- Drawing on Lefebvre and Foucault, LOS functions as a form of knowledge that reproduces power: it naturalises abstract, commodity-oriented space and marginalises the lived and perceived dimensions of urban life
- The capitalist state uses planning standards like LOS to manage urbanisation around production and consumption, employing commodification, naturalisation, regulation of everyday life, and uneven spatial development as its four primary spatial strategies
- The US 19 case study illustrates how LOS requirements produce wide, high-speed arterials hostile to pedestrians in intensely developed suburban corridors, demonstrating the human and urban design costs of applying highway standards to urban streets
- Contemporary transportation researchers and alternative design frameworks (VMT-based measures, NACTO Urban Street Design Guide, LEED ND) already offer technically sound alternatives, but their adoption is constrained by entrenched institutional and ideological structures

---

## Policy Implications and Recommendations

- Replace LOS with Vehicle Miles Traveled (VMT) as a primary service measure for urban streets, shifting the design goal from car throughput to reduced automobile dependence
- Adopt alternative design manuals — particularly the NACTO Urban Street Design Guide and LEED for Neighborhood Development — as recognised standards for urban and suburban street design in place of, or alongside, the AASHTO Green Book
- Reform federal, state, and local Traffic Impact Analysis requirements that mandate LOS compliance, which currently incentivise road widening and car-oriented development patterns
- Planning practitioners should recognise design standards as ideologically embedded instruments, not neutral technical tools, and engage critically with the knowledge frameworks that underpin transportation policy

## Advising Notes

This thesis is an excellent model for students interested in the intersection of transportation planning, planning theory, and critical urban studies, particularly those pursuing a Plan A thesis with a strong theoretical orientation. A student working in this tradition would need comfort with Marxist and critical social theory — including Lefebvre's spatial triad and Foucault's knowledge-power frameworks — as well as familiarity with transportation engineering concepts such as LOS, the Highway Capacity Manual, and traffic impact analysis. The research design is notable for its integration of three distinct bodies of literature into a coherent analytical framework applied to historical policy documents and a contemporary case study, offering a replicable model for theory-driven historical analysis in planning. Students drawn to advocacy planning, street-level equity, pedestrian and transit-oriented design, or critiques of technocratic planning practice will find this thesis particularly generative. It also demonstrates how Plan A research can engage directly with current policy debates — including 2025 federal executive actions on transportation — while remaining grounded in rigorous theoretical and historical analysis.
