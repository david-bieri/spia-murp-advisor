# Thesis: The Fiscal Resilience of American Cities

**Author:** Samuel S. Spencer

## Research Question and Problem Statement

How can fiscal health and resilience be operationalised into a quantitative model for assessing major American cities? The study examines whether certain cities are inherently more fiscally resilient than others by treating major recessions from 1977 to 2015 as exogenous shocks and tracking fiscal health indicators temporally. The ultimate goal is to produce a comparative tool that can inform policy actions aimed at improving municipal fiscal resilience.

## Geographic Focus

Major American cities nationally; longitudinal dataset spanning 1977–2015 drawn from the Lincoln Institute of Land Policy's Fiscally Standardized Cities (FiSC) database, covering 149 cities.

---

## Research Methods

- Quantitative fiscal ratio construction (10 ratios derived from municipal financial data)
- Index construction and composite scoring of fiscal health across four solvency dimensions (budgetary, long-term, short-term, service-level)
- Temporal analysis using major recessions as exogenous shocks
- Comparative analysis across city population cohorts
- Weighted indicator aggregation
- Literature review and synthesis of fiscal health and resilience frameworks
- Secondary data analysis using the Lincoln Institute FiSC database

---

## Key Findings

- Cities vary considerably in their fiscal resilience, with some demonstrating a stronger capacity to recover pre-recession fiscal health levels than others following economic shocks.
- No single fiscal ratio adequately captures resilience; meaningful assessment requires examining multiple solvency dimensions simultaneously — budgetary, long-term, short-term, and service-level.
- Revenue diversification emerges as a recurring factor associated with greater resilience, as cities heavily dependent on a narrow set of elastic revenue sources (e.g., property tax, sales tax) faced greater vulnerability during recessions.
- Many cities had still not fully recovered to pre-2008 recession fiscal health levels by the end of the study period, consistent with findings from parallel literature such as Pew Charitable Trusts (2013).
- Fiscal resilience cannot be assessed dogmatically; results must be contextualised within each city's local economic structure, revenue composition, and institutional environment.
- The quantitative model developed offers a replicable benchmarking tool for cities to compare their post-recession performance, though its value is best realised alongside qualitative local context.

---

## Policy Implications and Recommendations

- Cities should actively pursue revenue diversification strategies to reduce dependence on highly elastic or cyclically sensitive revenue sources, thereby building structural fiscal resilience ahead of future shocks.
- Municipal governments and policymakers should adopt standardised, ratio-based fiscal monitoring systems to track solvency trends over time and identify warning signs before crises emerge.
- Future fiscal resilience policy should incorporate accounting-theory perspectives — distinguishing between productive and unproductive uses of credit — to better connect local fiscal management to broader macroeconomic risk signals.
- The composite fiscal resilience index developed in this study offers a practical template for inter-city benchmarking; planners and finance officers should adapt such tools to local contexts rather than applying results prescriptively.

## Advising Notes

This thesis is an excellent model for students interested in the intersection of public finance, urban economics, and quantitative policy analysis — particularly those considering careers in municipal government, budget offices, or policy research organisations. A student undertaking a similar project would need proficiency in constructing and interpreting financial ratios, working with large longitudinal secondary datasets, and designing composite indices, making familiarity with Excel or statistical software (R, Stata) essential preparation. The thesis follows a clear Plan A structure: it develops a novel conceptual framework by synthesising two bodies of literature, constructs an original quantitative methodology, and applies it to a nationally significant dataset, making it a strong reference point for students designing empirically grounded Plan A theses. A notable research design feature is the deliberate acknowledgement of subjectivity in both "fiscal health" and "resilience" as concepts — Spencer explicitly frames quantitative results as tools for contextualised interpretation rather than objective verdicts, which models methodological humility appropriate for complex planning research. Students drawn to this thesis should be aware that working with standardised municipal financial databases requires careful attention to definitional consistency across jurisdictions, a challenge Spencer addresses directly and that any follow-on researcher would need to navigate.
