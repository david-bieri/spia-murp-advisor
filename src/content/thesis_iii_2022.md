# Thesis: An Empirical Assessment of Richard Florida's Creative Class Theory in the United States from 2001–2020

**Author:** John A. Washington III
**Year:** 2022
**Degree:** MURP — Plan A
**Advisor:** David S. Bieri

## Research Question and Problem Statement

Does Richard Florida's Creative Class Theory (CCT) accurately predict economic growth, measured as real per capita GDP, across U.S. metropolitan statistical areas over the 2001–2020 period? The study tests whether the cultural and occupational variables central to CCT — including bohemian indicators, creative class employment share, and tolerance proxies — bear a statistically significant positive relationship to productivity gains, or whether more conventional human capital and technology variables better explain observed growth patterns.

---

## Planning Domain

Economic Development; Urban Theory; Regional Planning

---

## Geographic Focus

United States — metropolitan statistical areas (MSAs) nationwide, with case study profiles of selected high-growth, low-growth, most-creative, and least-creative MSAs including San Jose CA, Midland TX, Boulder CO, Atlantic City NJ, and others.

---

## Research Methods

- Quantitative regression analysis (OLS) of MSA-level economic data
- Operationalization of Florida's Creativity Index variables into testable measures
- Longitudinal analysis of real per capita GDP growth across MSAs (2001–2020)
- Comparative case study profiling of MSA subgroups (high/low growth; high/low creative class share)
- Path model construction to illustrate hypothesized causal relationships among variables
- Secondary data analysis using federal economic datasets (BEA, BLS, Census)
- Literature synthesis across endogenous growth theory, urban economics, and economic geography

---

## Key Findings

- Creative class share of employment showed no statistically significant positive relationship — and in several model specifications a statistically significant *negative* relationship — to real per capita GDP growth from 2001–2020
- Higher education attainment levels, high-technology economic output (information, professional, and scientific services), and immigration from outside the U.S. showed substantial, positive, and statistically significant relationships to per capita GDP growth
- Florida's broader "Three T's" framework (talent, technology, tolerance) retains conceptual validity, but the operationalization of each component differs meaningfully from Florida's original model
- Bohemian cultural indicators — a distinctive element of CCT — were not supported as drivers of economic productivity
- Case study comparisons revealed that high-growth MSAs did not consistently exhibit high creative class shares, and high-creative-class MSAs did not consistently achieve high per capita GDP growth
- Findings align more closely with Glaeser's human capital critique of Florida than with Florida's own cultural-embeddedness argument, though Glaeser's use of population growth as a dependent variable is identified as a methodological limitation in the prior literature

---

## Policy Implications and Recommendations

- Economic development policy should prioritize expanding higher education access and supply and attracting or retaining degree-holders, rather than investing primarily in arts- and culture-based programming to attract the "creative class"
- Strategies to grow high-technology sectors — particularly information, professional services, and scientific industries — are more likely to produce per capita productivity gains than broad creative economy branding
- Immigration policy and local welcoming environments for international migrants represent an underutilized economic development lever at the regional level
- Future policy research should investigate complementary factors — including infrastructure quality, access to capital, housing affordability, and federal regulatory environments — that may amplify the effects of education, technology, and immigration on regional growth

## Advising Notes

This thesis is well suited as a model for students interested in quantitative economic development research who wish to critically evaluate influential planning theories against longitudinal empirical evidence. A student pursuing this path should be comfortable with regression analysis and secondary data management, and should have — or plan to develop — familiarity with federal economic datasets such as BEA regional accounts and Census occupational data. The thesis exemplifies the Plan A pathway in its use of an original quantitative research design, extended literature review situating the work within economic theory, and structured policy-relevant conclusions. A notable design feature is the deliberate disaggregation of Florida's composite Creativity Index into component variables, allowing the analysis to isolate which elements of CCT carry explanatory power and which do not — a methodological approach that could serve as a model for students evaluating other composite indices or scoring frameworks in planning practice.
